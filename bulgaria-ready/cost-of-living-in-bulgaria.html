Cost of living in Bulgaria

Bulgaria is still one of the cheapest countries in the EU to live in — but the gap has narrowed, and "cheap" covers everything from €700 a month in a village to €2,400 in central Sofia. Here's the honest spread, in euro.

Rents didn't jump at the euro changeover

Bulgarian leases were commonly written in euro long before January 2026, so the switch changed the label rather than the number. Retail and hospitality prices are a different story — plenty crept up during the transition, which is the usual pattern when a country changes currency.

That gap between the median (~€945) and the average (~€1,475) is the most useful thing on this page. Most "average salary in Bulgaria" articles quote the average, which describes a Sofia software developer rather than the person serving you coffee in Plovdiv. Judge local prices against the median.

The practical upshot: a couple on two modest UK state pensions are, in Bulgarian terms, comfortably above the local median household income. That buys a good deal of quality of life — and it's also why you should expect foreigner pricing on building work and second-hand cars unless you have a local with you.

What's genuinely cheap

  • Property. The headline attraction, and still true — village houses sell for sums that don't buy a car in Ireland.
  • Eating and drinking out. A proper restaurant meal for two with wine remains a fraction of UK prices.
  • Fresh produce in season. Markets are excellent and cheap. Tomatoes in August are a different food to what you're used to.
  • Public transport. City transit and intercity buses cost very little.
  • Trades and services. Labour is cheap by Western European standards — though see the warning above about foreigner pricing.
  • Income tax. A flat 10% rate, one of the lowest in the EU.

What isn't as cheap as you expect

  • Winter heating. Bulgarian winters are properly cold and older village houses are frequently uninsulated. Heating a draughty stone house can cost more than you'd pay in a modern Irish semi.
  • Cars and fuel. Fuel is broadly at EU levels, and used cars are not the bargain people expect.
  • Imported and branded goods. Anything shipped in — electronics, familiar British brands, specialist food — costs the same or more than at home.
  • Serious healthcare. Routine care is inexpensive, but many foreign residents end up paying for private cover to skip the state system's queues and language barrier.
  • Renovation. The cheap house is cheap for a reason. Rewiring, roofing, plumbing and damp work on an old village property regularly cost multiples of the purchase price.

The renovation trap

The single most common expensive mistake is buying a €20,000 village house and discovering it needs €50,000 of work to be habitable through a Bulgarian winter. Budget for a survey before you buy, not after — and get quotes from more than one builder.

Common questions

Cost of living, briefly

Can you live in Bulgaria on £1,000 a month?

Outside Sofia, yes — comfortably if you own your home, more carefully if you're renting. In central Sofia it would be tight. A couple who have bought outright in a village can live well below that.

Did prices rise when Bulgaria adopted the euro?

The conversion rate was fixed at 1.95583 leva to the euro, so nothing changed arithmetically. In practice retail and hospitality prices crept up during the changeover, as they have in every country that has switched. Rents were largely unaffected because leases were already quoted in euro.

Is healthcare free in Bulgaria?

There's a state system funded by contributions, and access depends on your residency and contribution status rather than simply on being there. Many foreign residents carry private insurance as well — it's inexpensive by Western European standards and removes both the queues and much of the language problem. Check your own entitlement carefully before you rely on it.

What's the income tax rate?

A flat 10% on personal income, among the lowest in the EU. Where you're tax resident, and how your pension or foreign income is treated, is a more complicated question than the headline rate suggests — take advice from a Bulgarian accountant before assuming it applies to you.
