## A note on voice

These are written as **someone researching a move, not someone who has made it.** That
is the honest position, and it is also the stronger one — expat groups spot borrowed
experience immediately, and one "so where are you based?" reply undoes the whole thing.

"I'm planning this and here's what I've dug up" invites people to correct and add to
you, which is exactly the engagement you want. Claiming to have lived it invites
scrutiny you can't survive.

If you'd rather not reference your own move at all, every post below works with that
line simply removed.

## Read this before posting anything

**Most Bulgaria expat groups ban promotional links.** Some ban them outright, some
require admin approval, some allow them only on a set day. Getting this wrong loses you
the group permanently, and there aren't many good ones.

1. **Check the pinned rules before your first post in any group.** If links are
   restricted, message an admin and ask. One yes is worth more than ten deleted posts.
2. **Never paste the same text into two groups.** Membership overlaps heavily and
   Facebook flags repeated text as spam. Every post below is written differently.
3. **Put the link in the first comment**, not the post. Reach is better too — Facebook
   suppresses posts with outbound links.
4. **2–3 groups a week, maximum.**
5. **Answer every comment.** A post with twelve replies from you beats three posts with
   none.

**Group types:** British/Irish expats in Bulgaria; buying property in Bulgaria; moving
to Bulgaria; region-specific groups (Veliko Tarnovo, Burgas, Sliven, Dobrich have
established British communities); Bulgarian language learners; retiring abroad.

Language-learner groups are the softest landing — not commercial, and a free alphabet
guide is squarely on-topic.

### Post 2 — The nod
*Best for: general expat groups, newcomer groups. No link at all — ideal first post in a new group.*

> One for anyone newly arrived, or arriving soon: in Bulgaria, **shaking your head means
> yes and nodding means no.**
>
> The genuinely confusing part isn't the gesture itself. It's that Bulgarians who deal
> with foreigners often switch to the Western version to be helpful — so you can never
> be sure which system you're in, and neither can they.
>
> The fix seems to be to stop reading the gesture and ask for the word. **Да** (da) is
> yes, **не** (ne) is no. Especially any time money or a decision is involved.
>
> Has anyone here agreed to something they absolutely did not mean to agree to?

**Link:** none. Pure goodwill — let it do its job.

### Post 6 — Go in February
*Best for: property groups, people at the thinking-about-it stage. No link needed.*

> The advice that comes up most often when I ask people who've already done this:
> **go back in winter before you buy.**
>
> Bulgaria isn't the Mediterranean. It's a continental climate — hot dry summers, and
> proper cold with real snow inland and in the mountains. Most people view in summer,
> when every village looks idyllic.
>
> The things you apparently only find out in February:
>
> - Whether the road is ploughed
> - Whether the shop is open, or shut till May
> - Whether anyone actually lives there in winter, or it's a summer village
> - What it costs to heat a stone house with no insulation
> - Whether the water and power hold up
>
> A trip in August tells you the house is lovely. A trip in February tells you whether
> you could live in it.
>
> For those who did buy — did a winter visit change your mind about anywhere?

### Post 7 — The renovation gap
*Best for: property and renovation groups. Links to /renovation, which is live.*

> Something that comes up again and again in this group and others: buying a €20,000
> village house and finding it needs several times that to be properly habitable through
> a Bulgarian winter.
>
> "Needs some work" in a listing can mean a new roof, a rewire, new plumbing and damp
> treatment. Any one of those can cost more than the house did.
>
> Two things people consistently recommend:
>
> 1. **Get a survey before you buy, not after.** Small cost against the number it can
>    save you.
> 2. **Get quotes from more than one builder**, ideally with someone local present.
>
> The cheap house is cheap for a reason, and the reason is usually the roof.
>
> Genuinely curious — what did people's renovations come in at against the original
> estimate?

### Post 3 — The land trap
*Best for: property buying groups, British expat groups. Unlocks as soon as the draft article is published.*

> Something worth checking before you pay a deposit, because it seems to catch British
> buyers repeatedly.
>
> Since Brexit, UK citizens are non-EU nationals in Bulgaria. You can own **an apartment
> or a building** in your own name with no special permit. What you cannot own in your
> own name is **the land underneath it.**
>
> For an apartment that's irrelevant — you own the unit, not the ground. For a village
> house with a garden it matters a great deal. The land normally has to be held by a
> Bulgarian company, usually an EOOD, which is a real and entirely legal route but comes
> with setup costs and annual filings that listings don't mention.
>
> Irish and other EU passport holders aren't affected and can own land outright.
>
> Agricultural and forest land is restricted regardless of nationality.
>
> None of this stops you buying. It just needs to be a conversation with your own
> Bulgarian lawyer *before* you agree a price, not after.
>
> **This isn't legal advice** — get your own lawyer, and not the one the agent
> recommends. If anyone here has been through the EOOD route, I'd genuinely like to hear
> how it went.

**Link (first comment):** `bulgariaready.com/guides/moving-to-bulgaria-from-uk`

### Post 1 — The six letters that catch everyone out
*Best for: language learner groups, general expat groups. Can run without the link if you want it out sooner.*

> Six Bulgarian letters look exactly like letters we already know, and none of them
> sound like it. They seem to be the reason street signs feel impossible at first:
>
> **В** is V, not B — *Вход* is "vhod", entrance
> **Н** is N, not H — *Не* is "ne", no
> **Р** is a rolled R, not P — *Ресторант*, restaurant
> **С** is S, not C — *Сметка*, the bill
> **У** is OO, not Y — *Улица*, street
> **Х** is a throaty H, not X — *Хляб*, bread
>
> And one with no English equivalent at all: **Ъ** is a vowel, a short "uh" like the u
> in "curt". It turns up immediately — **България** is "Bul-ga-ria".
>
> Once those seven click, most signs stop being a wall of symbols. I'm still working
> through it ahead of moving over next year.
>
> Which one caught you out the longest?

### Post 5 — Sofia isn't Bulgaria
*Best for: cost-of-living discussions, retiring abroad groups. Figures below are NSI-sourced.*

> A thing worth knowing when you read "average salary in Bulgaria" figures.
>
> The national average gross wage was **around €1,475 a month in March 2026** (National
> Statistical Institute). But **Sofia on its own runs around €1,732** — so the national
> figure is pulled up hard by the capital, and the typical wage away from Sofia is
> meaningfully lower again.
>
> Which matters practically, because it's part of why a builder's quote to a foreigner
> sometimes looks nothing like the quote a neighbour gets. Local prices are set against
> local wages, not against the national headline.
>
> Rough version: a couple on two modest UK state pensions are, in Bulgarian terms, doing
> well. That buys a lot. It also means it's worth having someone local ask the price
> first on building work and second-hand cars.
>
> Anyone want to sanity-check that against what you actually see where you are?

**Link (first comment):** `bulgariaready.com/guides/cost-of-living-in-bulgaria`

### Post 8 — What the euro actually changed
*Best for: general expat groups, anyone budgeting.*

> Since the euro changeover in January, a few things people still ask about:
>
> **Rents didn't jump.** Bulgarian leases were commonly written in euro long before
> adoption, so the switch changed the label rather than the number.
>
> **Retail and hospitality did creep up.** That's happened in every country that's
> changed currency, and it's the main reason things feel pricier than the fixed rate
> suggests.
>
> **The rate was fixed at 1.95583 leva**, so nothing changed arithmetically.
>
> **The lev stopped being legal tender on 1 February 2026**, though the central bank
> exchanges old notes and coins free of charge and with no deadline — so anything in a
> drawer isn't lost.
>
> Upside for those of us still planning a move: comparing prices with home is far easier
> than it used to be.
>
> For anyone who was there through it — did you notice prices move much in practice?
