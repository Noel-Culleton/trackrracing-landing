# Facebook group posts — 3-week rotation

Eight posts, each a different angle, for 2–3 groups a week. Every one stands on its own
as something useful even if nobody clicks. That is deliberate: it is the only version of
this that survives group moderation.

---

## Read this before posting anything

**Most Bulgaria expat groups ban promotional links.** Some ban them outright, some
require admin approval, some allow them only on a set day. Getting this wrong loses you
the group permanently, and there aren't many good ones.

Five rules:

1. **Check the pinned post and rules before your first post in any group.** If links are
   restricted, message an admin and ask. Being told yes once is worth more than ten
   posts that get deleted.
2. **Never paste the same text into two groups.** Membership overlaps heavily, and
   Facebook's own spam detection flags repeated text across groups. Every post below is
   written differently for that reason.
3. **Put the link in the first comment, not the post**, wherever rules are unclear.
   Reach is better too — Facebook suppresses posts with outbound links.
4. **2–3 groups a week, maximum.** More reads as marketing, whatever you write.
5. **Answer every comment.** A post with twelve replies from you outperforms three posts
   with none, and it is how people start recognising the name.

**Group types to target:** British/Irish expats in Bulgaria; buying property in
Bulgaria; moving to / relocating to Bulgaria; region-specific groups (Veliko Tarnovo,
Burgas, Sliven, Dobrich areas have established British communities); Bulgarian language
learners; retiring abroad / retiring in Europe.

The language-learner groups are the softest landing — they are not commercial, and a
free alphabet guide is exactly on-topic there.

## Week 1

### Post 1 — The six letters that catch everyone out
*Best for: language learner groups, general expat groups*

> Six Bulgarian letters look exactly like letters we already know, and none of them
> sound like it. They're the reason street signs feel impossible for the first week:
>
> **В** is V, not B — *Вход* is "vhod", entrance
> **Н** is N, not H — *Не* is "ne", no
> **Р** is a rolled R, not P — *Ресторант*, restaurant
> **С** is S, not C — *Сметка*, the bill
> **У** is OO, not Y — *Улица*, street
> **Х** is a throaty H, not X — *Хляб*, bread
>
> And one with no English equivalent at all: **Ъ** is a vowel, a short "uh" like the u
> in "curt". You meet it straight away — **България** is "Bul-ga-ria".
>
> Once those seven click, most signs stop being a wall of symbols. Took me far longer
> than it should have to work that out.
>
> Which one caught you out longest? Mine was С — I read *Сметка* as "cmetka" for months.

### Post 2 — The nod
*Best for: general expat groups, newcomer groups. High comment rate.*

> A reminder for anyone newly arrived: in Bulgaria, **shaking your head means yes and
> nodding means no.**
>
> The genuinely confusing part isn't the gesture. It's that Bulgarians who deal with
> foreigners often switch to the Western version to be helpful — so you can never be
> certain which system you're in, and neither can they.
>
> The fix is to stop reading the gesture and ask for the word. **Да** (da) is yes,
> **не** (ne) is no. Worth doing any time money or a decision is involved.
>
> Has anyone else agreed to something they absolutely did not mean to agree to?

**Link:** none. This one is pure goodwill — let it do its job.

## Week 2

### Post 3 — The land trap
*Best for: property buying groups, British expat groups. The most valuable thing you know.*

> Something worth checking before you pay a deposit, because it catches British buyers
> repeatedly.
>
> Since Brexit, UK citizens are non-EU nationals in Bulgaria. You can own **an apartment
> or a building** in your own name with no special permit. What you cannot own in your
> own name is **the land underneath it.**
>
> For an apartment that's irrelevant — you own the unit, not the ground. For a village
> house with a garden it matters a great deal. The land normally has to be held by a
> Bulgarian company, usually an EOOD, which is a real and entirely legal route but comes
> with setup costs and annual filings nobody mentions in the listing.
>
> Irish and other EU passport holders aren't affected and can own land outright.
>
> Agricultural and forest land is restricted regardless of nationality.
>
> None of this stops you buying. It just needs to be a conversation with your own
> Bulgarian lawyer *before* you agree a price, not after.
>
> Not legal advice — get your own lawyer, and not the one the agent recommends.

**Link (first comment):** the passport comparison → `bulgariaready.com/guides/moving-to-bulgaria-from-uk`

### Post 4 — Twelve signs
*Best for: language groups, newcomer groups. Very screenshot-friendly.*

> If you're arriving soon, these twelve words cover most of what's written on a door or
> a shopfront. Learn these and the first week gets noticeably easier:
>
> ВХОД — vhod — entrance
> ИЗХОД — izhod — exit
> ОТВОРЕНО — otvoreno — open
> ЗАТВОРЕНО — zatvoreno — closed
> АПТЕКА — apteka — pharmacy
> БАНКА — banka — bank
> ПОЩА — poshta — post office
> БОЛНИЦА — bolnitsa — hospital
> ТОАЛЕТНА — toaletna — toilet
> СПИРКА — spirka — bus stop
> ЦЕНТЪР — tsentar — town centre
> ХЛЯБ — hlyab — bread
>
> Screenshot it for your phone. АПТЕКА and БОЛНИЦА are the two you want to recognise
> instantly rather than work out.

## Week 3

### Post 5 — Median, not average
*Best for: cost-of-living discussions, retiring abroad groups*

> Every "cost of living in Bulgaria" article quotes the average salary, around €1,475 a
> month. That number is close to useless.
>
> The **median** is around €945. The average is dragged up hard by Sofia tech and
> finance, so it describes a software developer, not the person serving you coffee in
> Plovdiv.
>
> Judge local prices against the median and everything makes more sense — including why
> a builder's quote to a foreigner sometimes looks nothing like the quote a neighbour
> gets.
>
> Practical version: a couple on two modest UK state pensions are comfortably above
> local median household income. That buys a lot. It also means you should expect
> foreigner pricing on building work and second-hand cars unless you've got someone
> local asking the price first.

**Link (first comment):** full breakdown in euro → `bulgariaready.com/guides/cost-of-living-in-bulgaria`

### Post 6 — Go in February
*Best for: property groups, people at the "thinking about it" stage*

> The single best piece of advice I can give anyone viewing property here: **go back in
> winter before you buy.**
>
> Bulgaria isn't the Mediterranean. It's a continental climate — hot dry summers, and
> proper cold with real snow inland and in the mountains. Most people view in summer,
> when every village looks idyllic.
>
> The things you can only find out in February:
>
> - Whether the road is ploughed
> - Whether the shop is open, or shut till May
> - Whether anyone actually lives there in winter, or it's a summer village
> - What it costs to heat a stone house with no insulation
> - Whether the water and power hold up
>
> A viewing trip in August tells you the house is lovely. A trip in February tells you
> whether you can live in it.

**Link:** none, or the living guide in a comment if someone asks.

### Post 7 — The renovation gap
*Best for: property and renovation groups*

> The most expensive mistake I see repeated: buying a €20,000 village house and finding
> it needs €50,000 to be properly habitable through a Bulgarian winter.
>
> "Needs some work" in a listing can mean a new roof, a rewire, new plumbing and damp
> treatment. Any one of those can cost more than the house did.
>
> Two things that help:
>
> 1. **Get a survey before you buy, not after.** It is a small cost against the number
>    it can save you.
> 2. **Get quotes from more than one builder**, ideally with a local present.
>
> The cheap house is cheap for a reason, and the reason is usually the roof.
>
> What did people's renovations actually come in at against the original estimate?

**Link (first comment):** `bulgariaready.com/renovation`

### Post 8 — What the euro actually changed
*Best for: general expat groups, anyone budgeting*

> Since the euro changeover in January, a few things people still ask about:
>
> **Rents didn't jump.** Bulgarian leases were commonly written in euro long before
> adoption, so the switch changed the label rather than the number.
>
> **Retail and hospitality did creep up.** That's happened in every country that's
> changed currency, and it's the main reason things feel pricier than the fixed
> conversion rate suggests.
>
> **The rate was fixed at 1.95583 leva**, so nothing changed arithmetically.
>
> **The lev stopped being legal tender on 1 February 2026**, though the central bank
> exchanges old notes and coins indefinitely and free of charge, so anything in a drawer
> isn't lost.
>
> Upside: comparing prices with home is far easier than it used to be.

## Which to lead with

If you only run three, run **1, 3 and 4**.

- **1** and **4** are language posts. They're the least commercial, the most shareable,
  and the safest in strict groups.
- **3** is the land trap. It's the highest-value thing on the whole site and nobody else
  is saying it plainly.

Save **2** (the nod) for a group where you want goodwill before you ever post a link —
it has no link at all, which is exactly why it works as a first post somewhere new.

## Tracking whether it's working

Add `?ref=fb` to links if you want to see it in analytics later — though the site
currently runs no analytics, so for now use Search Console impressions on
`/guides/bulgarian-alphabet` and `/practice` as the signal. Give it three to four weeks
before judging; new pages don't rank instantly even at difficulty 0.
