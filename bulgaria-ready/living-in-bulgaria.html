Living in Bulgaria

Most articles about this are written either by estate agents or by people three weeks into the honeymoon. This is the version that covers the bureaucracy, the winters and the isolation as well as the cheap wine and the mountains.

The short version

What people love

  • Cost. Your money goes dramatically further, especially if you've sold property at UK or Irish prices.
  • Space. Land and house sizes that would be unaffordable at home.
  • Food. Markets, seasonal produce and a genuine food culture.
  • Landscape. Mountains, forest, the Black Sea, and very little of it overdeveloped.
  • Pace. Slower, less pressured, more time outdoors.
  • EU and Schengen. Full membership since January 2025 means no border checks travelling home.

What grinds

  • Bureaucracy. Slow, paper-based, and frequently requires you to appear in person with documents you didn't know existed.
  • Language. Outside cities and tourist areas, English is genuinely rare.
  • Winters. Properly cold, and rural houses are often uninsulated.
  • Rural isolation. Village populations are ageing and shrinking. Some are very quiet indeed.
  • Healthcare access. Fine in cities, a long drive from a serious hospital in the mountains.
  • Getting things done. Tradespeople, deliveries and officials all run on a different timescale.

The climate is not the Mediterranean

This catches people out more than anything else. Bulgaria has a continental climate: hot, dry summers and cold winters with real snow, particularly inland and in the mountains. The Black Sea coast is milder but has its own problem — resort towns that are lively in August and substantially shut from October to May.

If you're viewing in June, make yourself imagine the same village in February, with the shop shut and the road unploughed. Plenty of people buy a summer idyll and discover they've bought a winter one too.

Bureaucracy is the real adjustment

Not corruption, particularly — just process. Things that are a two-minute online form in Ireland or the UK are, in Bulgaria, an office visit, a queue, a stamped paper, and often a second office visit because the first office needed something the second one issues.

Two things make it survivable: a good local lawyer or fixer, and patience. Both are worth paying for. Trying to do residency paperwork, utility transfers and property registration yourself, in Bulgarian, from a standing start is a way to lose months.

Language decides how well this goes

You can get by in Sofia, Plovdiv, Varna and the resorts without Bulgarian. You cannot really get by in a village. Not because people are unfriendly — the opposite, generally — but because the person who can fix your roof, the woman at the council desk and your nearest neighbour simply don't speak English.

The people who settle happily are almost always the ones who made a real attempt at the language. You don't need fluency. Reading the alphabet and holding a hundred words changes how you're treated more than you'd expect.

The head-shake

Bulgarians traditionally shake their head for yes and nod for no. People who deal with foreigners often switch to the Western version, so you can never be certain which you're getting. When something matters, ask for the word rather than reading the gesture.

Healthcare: fine until it isn't

Routine care is inexpensive and generally competent, and pharmacies are excellent — Bulgarian pharmacists handle a lot of what would be a GP appointment at home. Most foreign residents take out private cover anyway, because it's cheap by Western European standards and removes both the queues and the language barrier.

The thing to plan around is distance. A picturesque mountain village is a long ambulance ride from a hospital equipped for a cardiac event or a stroke. If you're retiring, weigh that against the view honestly — it's the factor people most often wish they'd taken more seriously.

Community, and being the foreigner

There are established British and Irish communities, mostly near the coast and in particular inland areas. They're a genuine soft landing, and also a trap: it's entirely possible to live in Bulgaria for a decade inside an English-speaking bubble, never learn the language, and never quite belong anywhere.

Expect foreigner pricing on building work, second-hand cars and anything negotiated. It isn't personal and it isn't universal, but going in with a local who can ask the price before you speak will save you real money.

What's changed recently

  • The euro, January 2026. Bulgaria joined the eurozone at a fixed 1.95583 leva; the lev stopped being legal tender on 1 February 2026. Everything is now priced in euro, which makes comparison with home far easier.
  • Full Schengen, January 2025. No border checks by land, sea or air. Driving over with a loaded car, or popping home, is straightforward in a way it wasn't before.
  • Brexit, still biting. British citizens are non-EU nationals here, which changes residency and what you can own. Irish citizens are unaffected. See the passport comparison.
